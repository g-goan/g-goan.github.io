# The Art of Doing Nothing

*A seven-day experiment to find out what happens when we stop filling every empty moment.*

I have been wondering lately how much of my life I spend filling time.

Not necessarily doing anything important. Just filling it.

A few minutes while waiting for something. Pick up the phone.

Nothing to do? Check something.

A little bored? Scroll.

A quiet moment? Put something on.

Perhaps I am not unusual in this. We have become remarkably good at making sure that there is almost never a moment when we have nothing to do.

Our phones are always within reach. There is always another message, another video, another article, another piece of news, another thing to learn, another thing to buy, another person to see what they're doing.

There is always something.

And yet I've started wondering about the spaces between those things.

What happens when there is nothing?

And more importantly, why do I seem to have such a strong urge to make sure there isn't?

---

## I don't think I know how to do nothing anymore

When I say "doing nothing", I don't mean lying on a sofa for hours and refusing to do anything useful.

In fact, I'm not entirely sure what I mean by it yet.

Is sitting quietly and thinking doing nothing?

What about going for a cycle ride without a destination?

Sitting outside and watching the world?

Reading a book because I want to, rather than because I think I should learn something?

Following a completely impractical curiosity?

Writing down thoughts that may never become anything?

Taking a nap?

Having a long conversation?

Perhaps all of these are doing something.

Perhaps none of them are.

And maybe that's part of what I want to find out.

---

## The uncomfortable possibility

There is something else behind all this.

I sometimes feel guilty when I'm not being productive.

There is always that quiet thought:

*Am I using my time well?*

And underneath that:

*Am I fulfilling my potential?*

We seem to have become very good at measuring the value of our time by what it produces.

Work completed.

Things learned.

Tasks finished.

Goals achieved.

Money earned.

Progress made.

Even leisure can become strangely productive.

We exercise to become healthier.

We read to improve ourselves.

We listen to podcasts to learn.

We travel to have experiences.

We meditate to become calmer.

We rest so that we can become more productive afterwards.

Perhaps there is nothing wrong with any of that.

But what if wellbeing itself is a worthwhile use of time?

What if an hour can be valuable even when it produces nothing that can be measured?

And what if some of the things that look like wasted time are actually doing something inside us that we simply don't know how to measure?

I don't know.

That's the point.

---

## So I'm going to try something

For seven days, I'm going to experiment with **doing nothing**.

Not a digital detox.

I'm not throwing my phone away.

I'm not going to stop working.

I'm not going to become a monk.

And I'm definitely not going to spend seven days sitting motionless in a room trying to manufacture boredom.

The experiment is much simpler.

### Rule 1

When there is nothing particular to do, I won't automatically reach for my phone.

If I genuinely want to use it for something, I can.

The question is simply:

**Why am I reaching for it?**

And:

**What might I have done if the phone wasn't there?**

### Rule 2

Every day, I will deliberately create some unoccupied time.

There will be no fixed duration.

It might be fifteen minutes.

It might be an hour.

It might be longer.

I'm not interested in hitting a target and calling that success.

I want to create some space and see what happens inside it.

### Rule 3

When boredom appears, I won't immediately get rid of it.

I'll let it exist for a while.

And if something naturally emerges from it—a thought, a memory, curiosity, a walk, a book, a conversation, an idea, a desire to make something—I'll follow it.

The experiment isn't about forcing myself to do nothing.

It's about **not automatically deciding what should fill the empty space.**

---

## There won't be a prescribed way to spend the time

I can sit.

Think.

Daydream.

Write.

Read.

Walk.

Cycle.

Look out of a window.

Spend time in nature.

Talk to someone.

Make something.

Explore somewhere.

Learn something simply because I'm curious.

Rest.

Sleep.

Or do absolutely nothing.

The only thing I don't want to do is turn this into another productivity exercise.

I'm not going to measure how many useful thoughts I have.

I'm not going to count how many minutes of boredom I successfully endured.

I'm not going to award myself points for resisting my phone.

And I'm not going to declare the experiment a success because I discovered some profound insight.

If nothing happens, that's something worth knowing too.

---

## I want to pay attention

At the end of each day, I'll write down a few things.

Not a detailed spreadsheet.

Just whatever stands out.

Perhaps I'll notice how often I wanted to pick up my phone.

Perhaps I'll notice what triggered that urge.

Perhaps I'll remember something I had completely forgotten.

Perhaps I'll notice something around me that I normally wouldn't.

Perhaps I'll have an idea.

Perhaps I'll suddenly want to do something I've neglected for years.

Perhaps my mind will become clearer.

Perhaps it won't.

Perhaps boredom will turn into curiosity.

Perhaps it will simply remain boredom.

Perhaps I'll discover that I have been using constant stimulation to avoid something I don't particularly want to think about.

Or perhaps I'll discover nothing of the sort.

I'm deliberately leaving room for the experiment to surprise me.

---

## What I'm really curious about

There are two questions I already know I want to explore.

The first is:

**Has constant access to our phones changed our ability to tolerate boredom?**

I don't know the answer.

But I do know that the phone is almost always available when boredom appears.

And if the cure for every moment of boredom is immediate stimulation, perhaps we never get to find out what boredom becomes if we stay with it.

The second question is harder:

**Is wellbeing itself a legitimate use of time?**

Not rest because I need to recover so I can work harder.

Not relaxation because it improves my productivity tomorrow.

Not exercise because it will make me healthier.

Just wellbeing.

Feeling calm.

Feeling alive.

Feeling connected.

Having a clear head.

Enjoying an ordinary afternoon.

Can those things be enough?

I don't know.

And I'm not going to answer that question before I begin.

---

## Maybe “doing nothing” isn't really nothing

Perhaps that's what I'm most curious about.

Maybe when we stop filling every empty moment, something else gets a chance to happen.

A thought.

A memory.

A question.

A curiosity.

A connection.

A different way of seeing something ordinary.

Maybe we remember how to think our own thoughts instead of constantly consuming somebody else's.

Maybe we discover interests that got buried underneath everything that seemed more important.

Maybe we become more comfortable with ourselves.

Or maybe none of that happens.

Maybe I simply get bored.

Maybe I discover that I actually enjoy using my phone more than I thought.

Maybe I discover that some of the things I call "rest" are really just ways of avoiding being alone with my thoughts.

Maybe the whole idea of "doing nothing" turns out to be much more complicated than it sounds.

I'm willing to find out.

---

## Seven days

So that's the experiment.

For the next seven days, I'm going to create some empty space.

I'm going to notice the impulse to fill it.

I'm going to resist that impulse sometimes.

I'm going to follow genuine curiosity when it appears.

I'm going to pay attention to what happens when boredom is allowed to stay.

And I'm going to try not to turn the whole thing into another achievement.

I don't know what I'll discover.

That's actually what makes me want to do it.

And perhaps that's the first thing I've learned already:

**Not every use of time has to have a predetermined purpose.**

Maybe sometimes the purpose is simply to find out what happens when we stop deciding what the purpose should be.

I'll find out over the next seven days.

And I'll write about what happens.

Maybe you might want to try it too.

Not because I think I know what you'll discover.

But because I'm beginning to wonder what any of us might discover if, just for a little while, we stopped filling every empty moment.

And simply left some space.

**Let's see what happens.**
